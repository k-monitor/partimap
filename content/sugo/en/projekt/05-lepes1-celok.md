# Step 1: Defining precise needs and goals

Before creating the questionnaire, think through which problem you are trying to answer and what information you will really need.

A common mistake is trying to cover every possible topic with a single questionnaire. This results in a long questionnaire that is hard to complete, while the answers won't be focused enough either. A good questionnaire always concentrates on a clearly defined problem or decision.

## The 5 Whys method

The 5 Whys technique can be a great help in defining the problem precisely.

The essence of the method is to ask about a problem several times in a row: “Why did this happen?” Building on each answer in turn, you can gradually get to the real root of the problem.

The goal is not necessarily to ask the question exactly five times, but to keep following the line of reasoning until you reach a cause that you can actually influence.

## How to apply it

1. Define the problem.
2. Ask the question: Why?
3. Based on the answer, ask again: Why?
4. Repeat until you reach the real underlying cause.
5. Design the questions of the questionnaire based on the root cause identified this way.

**Example:**

Several of our earlier questionnaires dealt with the use of green spaces, so let's look at a related example.

**Problem:** The plants in the park are drying out.

#### 1. Why?

Because they don't get enough water.

#### 2. Why?

Because the irrigation system isn't working properly.

#### 3. Why?

Because maintenance wasn't carried out on time.

#### 4. Why?

Because the park maintenance team doesn't have enough capacity.

#### 5. Why?

Because the city budget doesn't allocate enough funds for maintaining green spaces.

**Root cause:** The maintenance of green spaces does not get enough priority and funding in the city budget.

This example shows well that the problem visible on the surface is often just a consequence. The 5 Whys method helps you get to the real causes that you can actually influence during the project.

## Separating causes from effects

In the planning phase, it's important to distinguish between causes and consequences. If you mix them up, you can easily end up looking for a solution to something other than the real problem.

**For example:**

- **Phenomenon:** Resident participation in local decision-making has increased in a city.
- **Cause:** A new participation tool was introduced, such as PARTIMAP.
- **Effect:** More proposals came in from residents, and decision-making became more transparent.

If you manage to uncover the real causes right at the start of the project, the questionnaire will be more targeted, and the results will be more useful as a basis for later decisions.
