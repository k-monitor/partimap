# Test your questionnaire

Always do a test run before publishing.

Have at least 5–10 people try out the questionnaire, and observe:

- how long it takes to complete;
- which questions they get stuck on;
- whether any wording is ambiguous;
- whether the branching works properly;
- whether it is comfortable to use on a mobile phone as well.

Based on the feedback from the pilot test, errors can be fixed before publication, which significantly improves the quality of the questionnaire.

## Online and offline surveys

When creating a questionnaire, it is important to decide whether the data will be collected online or in person (offline). The choice depends on the target group, the resources available, and the quality and quantity of data you need. Both methods have advantages and limitations, so it is worth considering which one best fits the purpose of your research.

### Online surveys

Online surveys can be accessed via an internet connection from a computer, tablet or mobile phone. PARTIMAP's survey module primarily supports this mode of data collection, which is particularly well suited to reaching large numbers of respondents quickly.

#### Advantages

- **Fast data collection:** the survey is available to many participants at once, and responses go straight into the database.
- **Lower costs:** there is no need for printing, postage or paper-based data entry.
- **Automatic data processing:** responses arrive in digital form, so errors from manual data entry are avoided.
- **Branching and conditional questions:** the system automatically shows only the relevant questions, so respondents see a shorter, personalized questionnaire.
- **Easy distribution:** the survey can be shared via email, social media, a QR code or a website.
- **Real-time monitoring:** incoming responses can be tracked continuously, so recruitment can be adjusted during data collection if necessary.

#### Disadvantages

- **Digital access required:** target groups without an internet connection or adequate digital skills are harder to reach.
- **Lower response rate:** without personal contact, more people may abandon or ignore the survey.
- **No immediate help available:** if a question is unclear, the respondent cannot ask for help right away.
- **Self-selection bias:** online surveys are often completed by people who are interested in the topic, so the results do not always represent the entire target group.

### Offline surveys

In offline data collection, the questionnaire is completed in person. This can be done on paper or on a digital device (for example, a tablet without an internet connection), with an interviewer providing help if needed.

#### Advantages

- **A wider target group can be reached:** people who rarely use the internet or digital devices can also be involved.
- **Higher response rate:** personal contact generally leads to greater willingness to participate.
- **Questions can be clarified:** the interviewer can help avoid misunderstandings, which can improve data quality.
- **Data collection in public spaces and at events:** can be used effectively at public forums, community events or site visits.
- **Greater control over sampling:** the researcher can more easily ensure that the questionnaire is completed by members of the intended target group.

#### Disadvantages

- **Higher costs:** printing, travel and hiring interviewers can require significant resources.
- **Longer data collection period:** approaching people in person is slower than distributing a survey online.
- **More errors can occur with paper-based data entry:** manual entry is time-consuming and increases the risk of typos.
- **The interviewer's presence can influence the answers:** on certain sensitive topics, respondents may answer less honestly in a face-to-face situation.

### Which method should you choose?

There is no solution that is ideal in every situation. An online survey is advantageous if you want to reach a large number of respondents quickly, the target group uses the internet regularly, and fast data processing is important. Offline data collection may be the better choice if you want to involve hard-to-reach target groups, you are aiming for a higher response rate, or personal assistance is needed.

In many projects, a combination of the two methods gives the best results. For example, in a local government development project, the survey can be available online on the city's website and social media pages, while at public forums or pop-up stands, tablets or paper questionnaires can be used to collect the views of those who take part in online surveys less often. A mixed-mode approach to data collection can help ensure that the responses received better reflect the views of the entire target group.
