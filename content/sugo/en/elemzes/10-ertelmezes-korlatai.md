# Limitations in interpreting the results

In data analysis, it is always important to consider the limitations of your results. The conclusions you can draw depend largely on how the data were collected and who filled in the survey.

If you are working with a non-representative sample, or members of the population under study did not have an equal chance of being included in the sample, you cannot generalize the results to the entire population.

For example, you cannot say that:

*“A quarter of the city's residents are satisfied with the city leadership.”*

Instead, you can say that:

*“A quarter of the survey respondents are satisfied with the city leadership.”*

That is why, when interpreting the results, you should always go back to the first step of the analysis and think through:

- Who filled in the survey?
- How did the survey reach them?
- How well do the respondents reflect the target group you want to study?

## Underrepresented groups

It is worth checking whether there are any groups that appear in the sample in smaller proportions than expected.

For example, if one third of a city's population is over 65, but only 10% of the survey respondents belong to this age group, the views of older people are probably not given due weight in the results. In such cases, you should point this out in the analysis and, if necessary, collect additional data.

A similar bias can arise if the survey was only available online. In this case, people without internet access are excluded from the survey from the outset, and the response rate is often lower, for example, among:

- older people,
- people with lower levels of education,
- or people less interested in the topic.

## Exceptional cases

There are also situations where you manage to reach every member of the population you want to study – or a large part of it.

This typically happens in smaller, well-defined communities. For example, if every member of an angling club receives the survey and almost everyone completes it, the results of the survey can truly reflect the views of the whole community.

## Be transparent

When presenting the results, you should always clearly state:

- who the target groups of the research were,
- how many respondents took part in the survey,
- through which channels the survey reached them,
- and what their demographic characteristics are.

This helps readers interpret the results correctly and understand the scope of their validity.
