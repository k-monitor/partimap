# Levels of measurement

In the previous example (Frequency and distribution chapter), we examined a nominal variable, that is, a variable whose values represent categories (for example, male or female). In surveys, however, you can come across many types of data. Some variables contain categories, others contain numbers (for example, age or a rating from 1 to 5).

Determining the type of a variable is important because it determines which statistical operations you can perform on it. In statistics, this is called the level of measurement, and it has four basic types:

- nominal,
- ordinal,
- interval,
- ratio.

## Nominal level of measurement

The values of nominal variables represent categories that cannot be put in order and have no unit of measurement. Therefore, you cannot perform mathematical operations on them; at most, you can calculate frequencies and percentage distributions.

**Examples:**

- gender,
- county or region,
- type of place of residence.

**Example:**

The survey includes the following question:

*“What is your gender?”*

If 53 of 100 respondents are women and 47 are men, you can say that:

- 53% are women,
- 47% are men.

## Ordinal level of measurement

With ordinal variables, the categories can be put in order, but the difference between them cannot be measured precisely. For this reason, you don't perform mathematical operations on these either.

**Examples:**

- level of education,
- type of settlement,
- satisfaction categories (for example, low–medium–high).

**Example:**

The survey includes the question:

*“What is the highest level of education you have completed?”*

Possible answers:

- At most 8 years of primary school
- Vocational school without a secondary school diploma
- Secondary school diploma
- Higher education degree

The order of the categories is clear, but you cannot say, for example, that a degree represents a “twice as high” level of education as a secondary school diploma.

If, out of 100 respondents:

- 18 have primary education,
- 23 completed vocational school,
- 35 have a secondary school diploma,
- 24 have a degree,

then you can conclude that the largest share of respondents (35%) have a secondary school diploma.

## Interval level of measurement

Interval variables take numerical values, and the differences between them are meaningful. They have a unit of measurement and can be added and subtracted, but they have no true, unambiguous zero point.

Examples include the 1–5 or 1–10 rating scales often used in surveys.

For such variables, you can calculate not only distributions but also averages.

**Example:**

The question:

*“How satisfied are you with the entertainment options in the town where you live? Rate them on a scale of 1 to 5!”*

Suppose you received 100 responses:

- 15 people gave a 1,
- 10 gave a 2,
- 40 gave a 3,
- 20 gave a 4,
- 15 gave a 5.

From this, you can determine the percentage distribution, but you can also calculate the average:

((15×1) + (10×2) + (40×3) + (20×4) + (15×5)) / 100 = 3.1

This means that respondents rated the entertainment options 3.1 on average.

## Ratio scale

The ratio scale is the highest level of measurement. Like the interval scale, it contains numerical values, but it also has an unambiguous zero point, so in addition to the four basic arithmetic operations, ratios are also meaningful.

**Examples:**

- age,
- income,
- number of years spent in education.

**Example:**

The question:

*“What is your age?”*

From the respondents' ages, you can calculate, for example:

- the average,
- the median,
- the standard deviation,
- or compare the average age of different groups.

For example, you can determine whether the average age of women is higher or lower than that of men, and also how large the difference between the two groups is.
